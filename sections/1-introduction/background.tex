\subsection{Background and Motivation}

Tourism is one of the largest industries in the world, yet it is also a phenomenon that is remarkably difficult to capture in a single model. Goeldner and Ritchie~\cite{goeldner2009} observe that ``\emph{tourism is a complex phenomenon, one that is extremely difficult to describe succinctly}'' (p.~14), and that any model of tourism must capture both its components and the processes and outcomes that occur within it. Within this system, the authors identify the tourist as the central element: ``\emph{The very heart of the tourism phenomenon model is unequivocally the tourist and the travel experiences that he or she seeks when visiting a tourism destination}'' (p.~15). Hotels, along with restaurants and conference centers, belong to the \emph{tourism superstructure}---facilities ``\emph{developed especially to respond to the demands of visitors}'' (p.~17)---meaning that every aspect of a hotel's design, service, and pricing must ultimately reflect the needs of travellers rather than those of local residents.

Within the tourism system, the accommodation (lodging) sector faces a challenge that is unique among retail industries: hotel room-nights are \emph{perishable inventory}. A room left unsold tonight represents revenue that is lost forever; it cannot be stocked and sold tomorrow. Combined with fixed capacity and pronounced seasonality, this characteristic creates the core business problem that revenue management addresses. The industry has responded: Goeldner and Ritchie note a clear trend toward ``\emph{the increased use of central reservation systems, emphasis on service, and the use of yield management techniques}'' (p.~190). Contemporary industry guides confirm that revenue management---``\emph{the strategic management of pricing, hotel inventory, and distribution channels}'' to maximise revenue and profits---remains a central concern for hoteliers~\cite{amadeus2023}.

This project focuses on hotel revenue management within the tourism domain because it is the sector where quantitative decision support can have the most immediate financial impact. According to the American Hotel and Lodging Association (AH\&LA) figures cited by Goeldner and Ritchie (p.~156), the U.S.~lodging industry alone encompasses 47{,}135 properties with 4.4~million rooms, generating \$133.4~billion in sales at an average occupancy rate of only 63.3\%. The remaining 36.7\% of unsold room-nights represents a substantial revenue opportunity that effective revenue management can help recover.

To ground this work in real data, we use the hotel booking demand dataset published by Antonio, Almeida, and Nunes~\cite{antonio2019}. The dataset contains 119{,}390 genuine booking records from two hotels in Portugal---a resort hotel (H1) in the Algarve region and a city hotel (H2) in Lisbon---covering arrivals from July~2015 to August~2017, including both realised and cancelled bookings. The two properties represent two distinct segments of the accommodation structure (resort vs.\ city centre), providing a realistic organisational context for the decision support system.

The course-adopted topic for this group is the \emph{Model Management Subsystem} (MMS) of a Decision Support System. Turban, Sharda, and Delen~\cite{turban2011} define the model base management system (MBMS) as ``\emph{a module that theoretically has capabilities similar to those of a DBMS to manage, manipulate, and run the models embedded in the DSS.}'' Unlike data management, where industry standards exist (SQL, relational model), there is no standard MBMS because of the hundreds of model classes, their varied uses, and the diverse solving techniques they require. This makes the MMS both the most challenging and the most interesting component to design, and it is the one we present in greatest depth.
