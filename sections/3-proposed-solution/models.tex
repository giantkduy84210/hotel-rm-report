\subsection{Model Base and Model Catalog}

\paragraph{From data to models.}
The models are not built on the raw CSV extract but on the warehouse of Section~3.2, which supplies three ingredients. First, \emph{per-night demand with segment detail}: \texttt{fact\_room\_night} gives, for every stay night, the room rate, the segment, and whether the stay occurred --- the basis for demand time series and for the group-versus-transient split. Second, a \emph{dense capacity snapshot}: \texttt{fact\_daily\_occupancy} gives rooms sold and estimated capacity for every (hotel, date, room type), including nights with zero sales --- the denominator of occupancy and the right-hand side of every capacity constraint. Third, the \emph{booking window}: lead times and reservation-status dates record when each booking was made and, when cancelled, when --- the basis for the cutoff-based show-up probabilities. Each model consumes a specific slice of this foundation; the paragraphs below state exactly what each model takes in, computes, and produces.

\subsubsection{The modelling framework}

\paragraph{The four variable types.}
The course requires every model to declare four variable roles~\cite{turban2011}. \emph{Decision variables} ``\emph{can be changed and manipulated by the decision maker, [and] correspond to the decisions to be made}''; \emph{uncontrollable variables (parameters)} ``\emph{affect the result of a decision but [are] not under the control of the decision maker}''; \emph{intermediate variables} ``\emph{contain the values of intermediate outcomes}''; and \emph{result variables} ``\emph{express the result of a decision, usually one of the goals of a decision-making problem}''. The book generalises this into the structure of every quantitative model (Figure~4.1): decision variables, uncontrollable variables, and parameters enter, are combined by mathematical relationships into intermediate variables, and produce the results~\cite{turban2011}. Every model below follows exactly this declaration discipline: for each model, Table~\ref{tab:m1vars}--\ref{tab:m5vars} assigns every quantity a role and a symbol before any prose description is given.

\paragraph{Influence diagrams: the formal definition.}
An influence diagram is ``\emph{a graphical representation of a model}'' --- ``\emph{a model of a model}''~\cite{turban2011} --- used ``\emph{to assist in model design, development, and understanding}'', in which ``\emph{influence refers to the dependency of a variable on the level of another variable}''~\cite{co4031slides}. The formal definition we follow is Shachter's~\cite{shachter1986}. An influence diagram is a network consisting of a directed graph $G = (N, A)$ with associated node sets and functions, whose node set $N$ is partitioned into three disjoint sets --- decision nodes $D$, chance nodes $C$, and value nodes $V$. There is at most one value node $v \in V$, drawn as a rounded rectangle, which represents the objective to be maximised in expectation. There are zero or more chance nodes in $C$, shown as ellipses, representing random variables (uncertain quantities). There may be zero or more decision nodes in $D$, drawn as rectangles, corresponding to the choices available to the decision maker. The arcs take their meaning from their target: arcs into utility and chance nodes are \emph{conditional} and represent probabilistic dependence (for deterministic intermediates: functional dependence); arcs into decision nodes are \emph{informational} and imply time precedence --- the source is known when the decision is made~\cite{shachter1986}. Figure~\ref{fig:idlegend} fixes the drawing conventions used for every diagram in this section.

\paragraph{Classification axes.}
Each model is classified on three axes~\cite{turban2011}. A \emph{static} model is solved once for a single decision situation, whereas a \emph{dynamic} model represents a process that evolves over time --- only M1 is dynamic, because demand is a time series whose forecast updates with each new observation. The certainty axis follows the decision conditions: under \emph{certainty}, inputs are taken as given (M3 receives point forecasts from M1, and the randomness is handled upstream); under \emph{risk}, outcomes are random but their distributions are estimable from data (M1's forecast errors, M2's demand distribution, M4's show-up probability); under \emph{uncertainty}, the distribution cannot be estimated and must be assumed (M5's price elasticity --- the dataset contains no independent price variation), which is why M5 reports a sensitivity analysis rather than a point claim. Independently, a model is \emph{deterministic} when its inputs are assumed known with certainty and \emph{probabilistic} when they are uncertain; linear programming is the textbook example of the former, forecasting and simulation of the latter. M3 is the only deterministic model in the suite; the other four are probabilistic.

\paragraph{Model categories and the management level.}
Turban et al.\ divide the model base into strategic, tactical, operational, and analytical models: strategic models ``\emph{support top managers' strategic planning responsibilities}''; tactical models are ``\emph{used mainly by middle managers to assist in allocating and controlling the organization's resources}'', with time horizons ``\emph{from 1 month to less than 2 years}'' and mostly internal data; operational models ``\emph{support the day-to-day working activities}'' with a daily-to-monthly horizon; and analytical models --- statistical, management science, data mining, financial --- ``\emph{perform analysis on data}''~\cite{turban2011}. All five of our models are \emph{tactical}: they set policies and limits over a booking horizon, executed by the operational layer, per Anthony's taxonomy. They are also \emph{analytical} --- statistical in M1's case, management science in M2--M4's.

\paragraph{The seven model groups.}
The book's taxonomy of quantitative models (Table~4.1) places each of ours in a group: M1 is a \emph{predictive model} (forecasting); M2 and M4 are \emph{optimisation via analytic formula} (closed-form critical ratios); M3 is \emph{optimisation via algorithm} (linear programming); M4's Monte Carlo verification is a \emph{simulation}; and M5 is analysis ``\emph{using a formula}'', the financial-modelling group. The structure follows the reference architecture of commercial revenue management systems, which execute explicit price optimisation, overbooking, and allocation in sequence, fed by demand forecasting~\cite{pimentel2019}: M1 supplies the forecasts, while M5, M4, and M2--M3 correspond to the pricing, overbooking, and allocation modules.
The suite also covers six of the seven functions that constitute ``\emph{all the functionality of a real-world revenue management system}'' in the simulation of Pimentel et al.~\cite{pimentel2019}; the exception---estimating true demand from censored demand---is handled as a documented limitation (Section~3.2).

\subsubsection{The five models}

\paragraph{M1 --- demand forecasting (dynamic, risk; predictive model).}
\emph{Business question:} how many rooms will the hotel sell each night over the coming weeks? \emph{Input:} the dense time series of rooms sold per stay date (from \texttt{fact\_daily\_occupancy}), optionally split by segment, together with the calendar attributes of \texttt{dim\_date} (day of week, month, holiday flags). \emph{Computation:} Holt--Winters exponential smoothing with weekly seasonality decomposes the series into level, trend, and seasonal components and extrapolates them $h$ days ahead. With $y_t$ the realised nightly demand, season length $s = 7$, and smoothing constants $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in (0,1)$ estimated in the fitting step, the three state recursions and the $h$-step forecast are
\begin{align}
L_t &= \alpha\,(y_t - S_{t-s}) + (1-\alpha)\,(L_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) && \text{(level)} \label{eq:m1level}\\
b_t &= \beta\,(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)\,b_{t-1} && \text{(trend)} \label{eq:m1trend}\\
S_t &= \gamma\,(y_t - L_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)\,S_{t-s} && \text{(seasonal)} \label{eq:m1seasonal}\\
\hat{y}_{T+h} &= L_T + h\,b_T + S_{T+h-s\lceil h/s \rceil} && \text{($h$-step forecast)} \label{eq:m1forecast}
\end{align}
The same series is also forecast by seasonal-naive baselines (repeat last week; same week last year scaled by recent growth). \emph{Output:} a point forecast $\hat{y}_{T+h}$ for each future date, plus the empirical forecast-error distribution from a rolling backtest. The evaluation discipline follows the field: all methods compete on the same backtest, and the winner is used --- not assumed (simple exponential smoothing was the most accurate method in four of eleven tested horizons in Ampountolas and Legg's multi-property study, as summarised in Yeoman et al.'s 2024 editorial~\cite{yeoman2024}).

\paragraph{M2 --- Littlewood group decision (static, risk; optimisation via analytic formula).}
\emph{Business question:} a group offers to book $G$ rooms for $n$ nights at a discounted rate $p_L$ --- accept or reject? \emph{Input:} the group request (rooms, nights, rate); the full transient rate $p_H$; the forecast of transient demand for the requested nights (mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, from M1); capacity $C$. \emph{Computation:} the classical two-class rule computes the protection level --- the number of rooms reserved for full-rate guests --- from
\begin{equation}
P(D_H > y^{*}) = \frac{p_L}{p_H},
\label{eq:m2rule}
\end{equation}
where $D_H \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the transient demand, so $y^{*} = \mu + \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(1 - p_L/p_H)$. The expected revenue of accepting (group revenue plus the expected transient revenue on the remaining rooms, $p_H \cdot \mathrm{E}[\min(D_H, C-G)]$, evaluated through the normal-loss function) is compared with the expected revenue of rejecting. \emph{Output:} the accept/reject recommendation, the expected-revenue difference, and the breakeven group rate. The rule is the original closed form of revenue management~\cite{littlewood2005}; its key assumption --- discounted demand arrives before full-fare demand --- is checkable in the data (group bookings have systematically longer lead times than transient bookings), and contemporary guidance frames the same decision as dynamic group pricing~\cite{amadeus2023}.

\paragraph{M3 --- allocation by linear programming (static, certainty; optimisation via algorithm).}
\emph{Business question:} several group requests compete for capacity across a week of multiple room types --- which to accept, and what is one more room-night worth? \emph{Input:} capacity per (night, room type) (from the warehouse estimate); the forecasted transient demand and rates per (night, type) (from M1 and the rate card); the set of group offers (rooms, nights, types, rates). \emph{Computation:} a mixed-integer linear program (continuous allocations, binary acceptances) over decision variables $y_{d,t}$ (rooms sold to transients per night and type) and $z_g \in \{0,1\}$ (accept group $g$):
\begin{equation}
\max \sum_{d,t} r_{d,t}\, y_{d,t} + \sum_{g} R_g\, z_g
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
y_{d,t} + \sum_{g \ni (d,t)} u_{g}\, z_g \le K_{d,t}, \quad
0 \le y_{d,t} \le \hat{D}_{d,t},
\label{eq:m3lp}
\end{equation}
solved with PuLP. \emph{Output:} the optimal revenue, the accepted set of groups, and the dual value of each capacity constraint --- the \emph{bid price}: ``\emph{the sum of dual variables across the proposed stayover nights}'', with the operating rule that ``\emph{a reservation request is accepted only if total room revenue exceeds the bid price}''~\cite{pimentel2019}. Because it takes point forecasts as given, the randomness of demand is handled upstream by M1 and M2 --- hence its certainty classification. Table~\ref{tab:lpcomponents} maps the six LP components of the course onto this program.

\paragraph{M4 --- overbooking (static, risk; optimisation via analytic formula, verified by simulation).}
\emph{Business question:} for a night with $C$ rooms, how many bookings $B$ should be accepted? \emph{Input:} capacity $C$; the show-up probability $p$, estimated at a cut-off $h$ days before arrival from bookings still on the books (the dataset records values as of the day prior to arrival~\cite{antonio2019}, and industry practice revises forecasts 7--30 days before arrival~\cite{jawabreh2026}); the room revenue $r$; the walk cost $c_w$, a management-set parameter --- the literature acknowledges this cost as ``\emph{subjective}''~\cite{pimentel2019}. \emph{Computation:} with $S \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(B, p)$ arrivals, the expected profit is $\pi(B) = r\,\mathrm{E}[\min(S,C)] - c_w\,\mathrm{E}[(S-C)^+]$; the optimal limit is the smallest $B$ satisfying the critical-ratio condition
\begin{equation}
P(S \geq C) \;\geq\; \frac{r}{r + c_w},
\label{eq:m4ratio}
\end{equation}
found by an exact binomial scan and cross-checked by Monte Carlo simulation with reported confidence intervals. \emph{Output:} $B^{*}$, the overbooking level $B^{*}-C$, the probability of walking at least one guest, and the expected number of walked guests. The critical ratio is the newsvendor structure shared with the first airline overbooking models~\cite{rothstein1971,talluri2004}.

\paragraph{M5 --- pricing by goal seeking (static, uncertainty; what-if and sensitivity by formula).}
\emph{Business question:} what nightly ADR is needed to reach a target RevPAR in a given season? \emph{Input:} the baseline occupancy and ADR of the season (from the warehouse: seasonal averages over realised stays); the price elasticity $e$; the target RevPAR. \emph{Computation:} the constant-elasticity demand response $\text{occ}(\text{ADR}) = \text{occ}_0 \cdot (\text{ADR}/\text{ADR}_0)^{e}$ induces $\text{RevPAR}(\text{ADR}) = \text{ADR} \cdot \text{occ}(\text{ADR})$; a root-finding solver (Brent's method) inverts this curve to find the minimum ADR that achieves the target --- well posed for $-1 < e < 0$, the range in which a rate increase still raises RevPAR. \emph{Output:} the required ADR, the occupancy at that point, and a tornado chart showing how the answer moves as each parameter varies. Its certainty class is \emph{uncertainty} in a precise sense: the dataset contains no independent price variation, so the elasticity cannot be estimated and must be set by the user as an explicit, visible assumption. The model therefore implements the goal-seeking mode directly --- ``\emph{calculates the values of the inputs necessary to achieve a desired level of an output (goal)}''~\cite{turban2011} --- and the tornado implements sensitivity analysis, which ``\emph{attempts to assess the impact of a change in the input data or parameters on the proposed solution}''.

\subsubsection{Model chaining and the analysis modes}

\paragraph{Chaining.}
The models compose exactly as the theory describes model integration --- ``\emph{directing the output of one model, say forecasting, to be processed by another one, say a linear programming planning model}''~\cite{turban2011} --- and the book's compact statement of the same pattern: ``\emph{often, the results of one type of model component (e.g., forecasting) are used as input to another (e.g., production scheduling)}'' (p.~98)~\cite{turban2011}. The hotel domain has its own documented instance: at Harrah's Cherokee Casino and Hotel, ``\emph{the forecasts are used by overbooking and optimization models for inventory-control recommendations}'' (App.~Case~4.2, p.~143)~\cite{turban2011} --- a forecast feeding overbooking and optimisation models, which is precisely the M1$\to$M3/M4 structure of Figure~\ref{fig:chaining}. The classic scale demonstration is P\&G's supply-chain DSS, whose six models --- among them a demand-forecasting model, a distribution-center location model, and a transportation linear program ``\emph{fed to it from the previous model}'' (pp.~139--140) --- were ``\emph{integrated by the DSS}'' (p.~143)~\cite{turban2011}. This is also why a DSS needs several models rather than one --- ``\emph{A DSS can include several models (sometimes dozens), each of which represents a different part of the decision-making problem}''~\cite{turban2011}: M1 answers what will happen, M2--M4 what to do with capacity, M5 what rate to set.

\paragraph{The three analysis modes.}
The interactive layer exposes all three analysis modes the theory defines~\cite{turban2011}: \emph{what-if analysis} (``\emph{what will happen to the solution if an input variable, an assumption, or a parameter value is changed?}'') --- every model parameter is overridable at run time, and the resulting KPIs recompute immediately; \emph{sensitivity analysis} --- M5's tornado chart varies each parameter and ranks their impact; and \emph{goal seeking} --- M5 itself. M4's Monte Carlo verification adds \emph{probabilistic simulation} --- ``\emph{a technique for conducting experiments (e.g., what-if analyses) with a computer on a model}''~\cite{turban2011} --- which is descriptive rather than normative: it does not choose $B^{*}$, it validates the choice.

\paragraph{What we deliberately do not use.}
Search methods (blind or heuristic) are unnecessary because every model has an analytical solution or a standard solver; multicriteria methods such as AHP are not needed because the goals reduce to one primary metric (RevPAR) plus a constraint; and no model attempts to automate judgment --- the semistructured portion of the decisions stays with the manager, as Section~2.3 argued.
