\subsection{Model Catalog}

The model base contains five models, M1--M5, covering the three decisions of Section~2: pricing is supported by M1 (demand forecasting) and M5 (pricing goal seeking), group-versus-transient allocation by M2 (Littlewood rule) and M3 (linear programming), and overbooking by M4. Following the modelling discipline of the course, every model declares its four variable roles---decision variables, uncontrollable variables, intermediate variables, and result variables---together with its classification on three axes: static versus dynamic, certainty versus risk versus uncertainty, and the management level of the decision it supports~\cite{turban2011}.
In Anthony's taxonomy all five support tactical-level (management control) decisions: they set policies and limits over a booking horizon, while the operational layer---the reservation system and the front desk---executes those limits per request. Table~\ref{tab:modelcatalog} summarises the catalog; the paragraphs that follow declare each model.
The structure follows the reference architecture of commercial revenue management systems, which execute explicit price optimisation, overbooking, and allocation in sequence, fed by demand forecasting~\cite{pimentel2019}: M1 supplies the forecasts, while M5, M4, and M2--M3 correspond to the pricing, overbooking, and allocation modules.
The suite also covers six of the seven functions that constitute ``all the functionality of a real-world revenue management system'' in the simulation of Pimentel et al.~\cite{pimentel2019}; the exception---estimating true demand from censored demand---is handled as a documented limitation (Section~3.2).

\paragraph{M1 --- demand forecasting (dynamic, risk).}
\emph{Decision variables:} the forecasting method, the seasonal period, and the forecast horizon. \emph{Uncontrollable variables:} the actual demand that will materialise, seasonality, and events outside the data. \emph{Intermediate variables:} fitted values, residuals, estimated seasonal components. \emph{Result variables:} the forecast of rooms sold per night, with the forecast error (MAE, RMSE, WAPE) measured on a rolling backtest. The primary method is Holt-Winters exponential smoothing with weekly seasonality (\texttt{statsmodels}); because the data covers only about two years, annual seasonality cannot be estimated within the model and is instead carried by same-week-last-year baselines. The model is \emph{dynamic} (it represents a process evolving over time) and operates under \emph{risk}: demand is random but its distribution is learnable from history. Forecasting is the one hotel revenue management task with the richest methodological literature~\cite{weatherford2003,song2008,peng2014}, and M1 deliberately follows the evaluation discipline of that literature: the smoothing method is compared against seasonal-naive baselines on the same backtest, and the method that wins is the one that is used---not the one that is assumed.
Recent field evidence supports this discipline: in a 2023 multi-property study of daily hotel occupancy prediction, simple exponential smoothing was the most accurate method in four of the eleven tested horizons---winning against far more complex gradient boosting despite requiring minimal statistical knowledge~\cite{yeoman2024}.

\paragraph{M2 --- Littlewood rule (static, risk).}
\emph{Decision variables:} the protection level $y$ (rooms reserved for full-rate transient guests) and the accept/reject decision on a group request. \emph{Uncontrollable variables:} transient demand $D$ per night, the full rate $p_H$, the group rate $p_L$, and capacity $C$. \emph{Intermediate variables:} the expected sales $\mathrm{E}[\min(D, x)]$ at each availability $x$. \emph{Result variables:} the expected-revenue difference between accepting and rejecting the group, and the breakeven group rate. The rule originates in airline seat control~\cite{littlewood2005} and is the classical two-class model of revenue management~\cite{talluri2004}: with low-fare demand arriving first and high-fare demand $D$ following a known distribution, the optimal protection level satisfies
\begin{equation}
  P(D > y^{*}) = \frac{p_L}{p_H}.
\end{equation}
The model is \emph{static} (one decision period) and operates under \emph{risk} (the demand distribution is estimated from the warehouse). Its key assumption---that discounted demand arrives before full-fare demand---is checkable in the data: group bookings have systematically longer booking lead times than transient bookings, which the warehouse confirms through the market-segment and date dimensions.
Contemporary industry guidance describes the same structure as \emph{dynamic group pricing}: group rates are adjusted with demand, competition, and the size and nature of the group, and group bookings have a different booking window from individual reservations~\cite{amadeus2023}.

\paragraph{M3 --- allocation as linear programming (static, certainty).}
\emph{Decision variables:} the number of rooms sold to transient guests per (night, room type) $y_{d,t}$, and one accept variable $z_g$ per group request. \emph{Uncontrollable variables:} capacity per (night, room type), forecasted transient demand, room rates, and the set of group offers. \emph{Intermediate variables:} shadow prices of the capacity constraints. \emph{Result variables:} the optimal revenue, the accepted set of groups, and the dual value of extra capacity on each night. The program maximises total revenue subject to capacity and demand constraints, and is solved with PuLP. Where M2 answers a single group request against a transient demand distribution, M3 answers the multi-night, multi-room-type version: several group offers competing for the same capacity across a week, which is the realistic form of the allocation decision. Because it takes point forecasts as given, it is a \emph{certainty} model at the optimisation layer; the randomness of demand is handled upstream by M1 and M2. Its result variables include the shadow prices---the marginal revenue value of one more room on a constrained night---which give the revenue manager exactly the sensitivity information that Section~2's problem statement asks for.
In the research literature this dual value is the \emph{bid price}---``the sum of dual variables across the proposed stayover nights''---with the operating rule that ``a reservation request is accepted only if total room revenue exceeds the bid price''~\cite{pimentel2019}.

\paragraph{M4 --- overbooking (static, risk).}
\emph{Decision variables:} the booking limit $B$ (equivalently, the overbooking level $B - C$). \emph{Uncontrollable variables:} the probability $p$ that a booking still on the books actually arrives, capacity $C$, the room revenue $r$, and the walk cost $c_w$. \emph{Intermediate variables:} the number of arriving guests $S \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(B, p)$, expected occupied rooms $\mathrm{E}[\min(S, C)]$, and expected walked guests $\mathrm{E}[(S - C)^+]$. \emph{Result variables:} the optimal limit $B^{*}$, the expected profit gain, the probability of walking at least one guest, and the expected number of walked guests. The optimal limit is the smallest $B$ satisfying $P(S \geq C) \geq r / (r + c_w)$, the critical-ratio structure shared with the newsvendor problem; the heritage of this model goes back to the first airline overbooking models~\cite{rothstein1971,talluri2004}, and its joint formulation with allocation is an active topic in the hotel literature~\cite{pimentel2019}. One measurement subtlety is decisive and is enforced in the design: $p$ must be estimated from bookings that are \emph{still on the books} $h$ days before arrival---not from the final status of all bookings---because most cancellations happen early, and using the final show rate would overstate the no-show risk and recommend excessive overbooking.
The dataset itself was constructed under the same principle: variable values were ``\emph{extracted from the bookings change log, with a timestamp relative to the day prior to arrival date}''~\cite{antonio2019}, so the data available to the model is already an on-the-books snapshot at $h = 1$.
The choice of cut-off horizons follows industry practice: conventional hotel pricing revises its forecasts 7--30 days before arrival~\cite{jawabreh2026}, so the design measures $p$ at several cut-offs in this window (e.g., 7, 14, and 30 days before arrival), tightening as the arrival date approaches.
In the terminology of the research literature, the booking limit sets the \emph{effective capacity}---the number of reservations deliberately held above the physical room count, net of expected cancellations and no-shows---and the walk cost that anchors it is acknowledged to be ``subjective'' in practice~\cite{pimentel2019}, which is why $c_w$ is exposed to the decision maker as an adjustable assumption rather than hidden. Because the warehouse never observes true capacity being exceeded (issue~2 in Section~3.2), M4 is presented as a decision-support tool with management-set parameters rather than as a validated revenue guarantee, and its output is cross-checked by Monte Carlo simulation with reported confidence intervals.

\paragraph{M5 --- pricing goal seeking (static, uncertainty).}
\emph{Decision variables:} the nightly ADR to charge in the target season. \emph{Uncontrollable variables:} the baseline occupancy and ADR, the price elasticity $e$, the capacity, and the target RevPAR. \emph{Intermediate variables:} the demand response $\text{occ}(\text{ADR}) = \text{occ}_0 \cdot (\text{ADR}/\text{ADR}_0)^{e}$ with $e < 0$, and the induced RevPAR. \emph{Result variables:} the minimum ADR that reaches the target, the occupancy at that point, and the tornado sensitivity of the answer to each parameter. The model inverts the demand curve with a root-finding solver---the \emph{goal seeking} mode of Turban et al.~\cite{turban2011}: instead of asking ``\emph{what happens if we set this rate?}'', the manager asks ``\emph{what rate do we need?}''. Its certainty class is \emph{uncertainty}, not risk, in a precise sense: the dataset contains no independent price variation, so the elasticity $e$ cannot be estimated from the data and must be set by the user as an explicit assumption. The model therefore reports its result together with a tornado chart showing how the answer moves as $e$ and the baselines vary, making the assumption's influence visible instead of hidden.

\paragraph{How the models work together.}
The catalog is a pipeline as much as a menu: M1 produces the demand forecasts (with uncertainty) that parameterise M2's demand distribution and M3's right-hand sides; M2 gives the quick single-request answer, M3 the full multi-night allocation; M4 protects the chosen allocation against cancellations; and M5 closes the loop on the primary goal of Section~2 by translating a RevPAR target into a rate. The models share one warehouse, one parameter interface, and one run log---the model management machinery to which we now turn.
