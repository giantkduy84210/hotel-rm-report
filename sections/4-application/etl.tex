\subsection{ETL Pipeline}

\paragraph{Extract.}
The source data arrives as two CSV files (one per hotel, H1 and H2) from the public release of Antonio et al.~\cite{antonio2019}. Each row is one booking with 31 variables; the two files share the same schema and are loaded with pandas, then concatenated with a hotel identifier added to distinguish the properties. The extraction step performs no transformations --- its only job is to read the source faithfully and record row counts per hotel for later reconciliation.

\paragraph{Transform.}
The transform stage performs five operations in sequence, each with its own validation:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Date assembly.} The four arrival-date components (year, week number, day of week, day of month) are combined into a single arrival date per booking. Bookings whose assembled date falls outside the data window (July~2015 to August~2017) are flagged and counted.
  \item \textbf{Stay explosion.} Each booking is expanded into one row per night of stay, using the weekend-nights and week-nights variables: a booking with 2 weekend nights and 3 week nights becomes 5 rows, each carrying the booking's rate, segment, and status. This produces the booking-night grain of \texttt{fact\_room\_night}.
  \item \textbf{Surrogate key encoding.} Each dimension attribute (date, hotel, room type, market segment, customer type, deposit type, distribution channel) is encoded as a surrogate integer key against its dimension table. NULL values in the agent and company columns are treated as the category ``not applicable'' --- not as missing data, per the source paper's specification.
  \item \textbf{Realised-revenue split.} For each booking-night row, a realised-revenue measure is set to the room rate if the booking was not cancelled, and zero otherwise. This implements the first data correction of Section~3.2: revenue and occupancy are computed only from stays that actually occurred. On the executed run the realised totals are EUR\,11{,}601{,}634 for H1 (ADR EUR\,96.83 on kept stays) and EUR\,14{,}385{,}342 for H2 (ADR EUR\,106.98).
  \item \textbf{Capacity estimation.} The number of rooms per hotel is not in the dataset; it is estimated as the maximum number of rooms sold on any single night, computed after a warm-up window that excludes the first weeks of data. The ETL recomputes this figure, asserts it against the realised-stay data, and stores it in \texttt{dim\_hotel} together with the estimation basis: \textbf{187 rooms for H1 and 226 for H2} (maxima of nightly rooms sold after the warm-up window ending 30 September 2015).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Cleaning log.}
Every cleaning rule records the number of rows it removes and the reason. The rules target anomalies documented in the source paper's summary statistics: negative ADR values (the minimum ADR is $-6.38$ for H1), extreme ADR outliers (a transparent cap at EUR\,1{,}000 per night, which covers the paper's maximum of 5{,}400 for H2), impossible party sizes (more than 20 adults, which covers the paper's maximum of 55 for H1; also bookings with zero guests of any kind), and stays with zero total nights. On the executed run the four rules removed 1, 1, 190, and 645 bookings respectively: \textbf{119{,}390 bookings in, 118{,}553 clean bookings out}, expanded to \textbf{408{,}381 booking-night rows}; the log is written to \texttt{cleaning\_log.json} alongside the warehouse so that any consumer can verify exactly how many rows were removed and why. Fixed-width string fields in the source (room types, segments) are also stripped of trailing spaces during cleaning.

\paragraph{Load and quality gates.}
The transformed data is loaded into the DuckDB star schema. The pipeline then runs a suite of quality gates, each of which must pass before the warehouse is accepted; on the executed run all six passed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Referential integrity:} no orphan foreign keys (0 violations).
  \item \textbf{Row-count reconciliation:} clean bookings equal distinct booking identifiers in the fact table (118{,}553 = 118{,}553).
  \item \textbf{Cancellation consistency:} \texttt{IsCanceled} = 1 if and only if \texttt{ReservationStatus} is ``Canceled'' or ``No-Show'' --- the identity verified in the data study ($11{,}122 = 10{,}831 + 291$ for H1; $33{,}102 = 32{,}186 + 916$ for H2); the executed run found 0 mismatches across all 118{,}553 bookings.
  \item \textbf{Revenue reconciliation:} booked revenue minus cancelled revenue equals realised revenue (0 residual per hotel).
  \item \textbf{Capacity assertion:} rooms sold never exceeds estimated capacity (0 violations over the 12{,}896 dense snapshot rows).
  \item \textbf{Density check:} the daily snapshot contains a row for every (hotel, date, room type), including zero-sale nights (12{,}896 rows, no gaps).
\end{itemize}

If any gate fails, the pipeline exits with a non-zero status and no partial warehouse is published.

\paragraph{OLAP demonstration.}
After the load succeeds, eight analytical queries demonstrate the warehouse's capability across the four classical OLAP operations: roll-up (hotel $\to$ year $\to$ month revenue), drill-down (month $\to$ market segment), slice (the Groups segment across both hotels), dice (hotel $\times$ season $\times$ year), show-up rate (hotel $\times$ season at cut-off $h = 7$: 0.912--0.972 depending on hotel and season --- the input of M4), group-vs-transient revenue share (hotel $\times$ season: from 0.5\% in H1's High season to 18.2\% in H1's Shoulder --- the seasonal policy space of M2/M3), day-of-week pattern (H1 averages 160.7 rooms on Saturdays against 142.8 on Sundays), and booking-window lead time (group bookings' median lead time is \textbf{162 days against 12 for Direct bookings} --- the empirical confirmation, in the warehouse, of the Littlewood assumption that discounted demand arrives first).
