\subsection{Problem Statement}

\paragraph{Organisational context.}
The organisation is a hospitality group operating two properties in Portugal: H1, a resort hotel in the Algarve, and H2, a city hotel in Lisbon~\cite{antonio2019}. The two properties serve different customer mixes and demand patterns---H1 is leisure-oriented with pronounced summer seasonality, while H2 has a steadier mix of business and city-break travellers. Revenue management decisions for each property are made by the revenue manager in consultation with the operations director.

\paragraph{Decision makers.}
The primary decision maker is the \textbf{revenue manager}, who sets rates, manages inventory allocation, and decides overbooking levels. The \textbf{operations director} is involved for capacity and staffing implications --- staffing schedules follow the same demand forecasts that drive the revenue decisions~\cite{jawabreh2026}. Both roles require interactive access to data and models, which the proposed DSS provides.

\paragraph{The three recurring decisions.}
Each decision is a trade-off between two ways of being wrong: pricing balances empty rooms against forfeited revenue, allocation balances guaranteed group revenue against higher-paying late arrivals, and overbooking balances empty rooms against walked guests.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pricing (tactical)}: setting nightly rates by room type and season. Business travellers pay \$112 per night on average versus \$103 for leisure travellers~\cite{goeldner2009} (p.~156); the revenue manager must decide how much to charge each segment on each date, knowing that pricing too high in low season leaves rooms empty while pricing too low in high season forfeits revenue.
  \item \textbf{Group vs.\ transient allocation (tactical)}: when a tour operator or corporate client offers to purchase a block of rooms at a discounted rate (e.g., 40 rooms at \$80 per night for a fixed period), the revenue manager must decide how many rooms to reserve for this group versus holding them for individual (transient) guests who may book at a higher rate (e.g., \$120---illustrative figures; the documented segment spread is \$103--\$112 per night, p.~156~\cite{goeldner2009}). The research literature poses allocation more broadly, as the division of effective capacity across rate classes~\cite{pimentel2019}; we focus on the group-versus-transient variant because the dataset carries an explicit Groups segment with a distinct booking window, industry guidance devotes a separate strategy to group business~\cite{amadeus2023}, and dedicated group-pricing systems exist in practice---notably Marriott's Group Pricing Optimizer~\cite{hormby2010}. This is an allocation problem under capacity constraints.
  \item \textbf{Overbooking level (tactical)}: because a predictable share of bookings is cancelled or results in no-shows, selling exactly to capacity guarantees unsold rooms. The manager must decide how many rooms to sell beyond capacity to maximise expected revenue, subject to the risk and cost of walking a guest to another hotel.
\end{enumerate}
In Anthony's taxonomy, all three are tactical (management control) decisions: the revenue manager sets policies and limits---rates, protection levels, booking limits---over a booking horizon, and the operational layer executes them, with the reservation system and front desk applying the limits to each request and handling the rare walk event on the night of arrival.

\paragraph{Why the problem is semistructured.}
Following Simon's framework as presented in the course~\cite{turban2011}, these decisions are \emph{semistructured}: the quantitative portion---forecasting demand, computing optimal allocations, evaluating overbooking expected values---can be handled by computer models. The qualitative portion---competitor actions, special events, brand positioning, relationship with group clients---requires human judgment --- and the field's own assessment agrees: machine intelligence and decision analytics ``\emph{cannot independently guarantee improved performance}'', because their value ``\emph{depends on their integration with human intelligence, managerial judgment, and business insight}''~\cite{jawabreh2026}.
A DSS is the appropriate paradigm precisely because it combines the computational power of models with the domain knowledge of the decision maker, aiming to support rather than replace the human.

\paragraph{Data.}
The decisions are grounded in the real booking dataset of Antonio et al.~\cite{antonio2019}: 119{,}390 bookings (40{,}060 at the resort, 79{,}330 at the city hotel) with 31 variables covering arrival dates, length of stay, room type, market segment, customer type, deposit type, average daily rate (ADR), and cancellation status, for the period July~2015 to August~2017.
The dataset fills a real gap in the field: most revenue management research uses aviation data in the Passenger Name Record (PNR) format, whereas ``\emph{the remaining tourism and travel industries like hospitality... cannot be fully explored without industry's specific data}''~\cite{antonio2019}.
