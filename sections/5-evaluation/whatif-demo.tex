\subsection{What-if Demonstration}

The interactive application is demonstrated through a sequence of scenarios that exercise all three analysis modes --- what-if, sensitivity, and goal seeking --- across the three decisions of Section~2.3. All numbers are the executed runs, logged in \texttt{fact\_scenario\_run} with their full parameter sets.

\paragraph{Scenario S1: cancellation-rate increase (what-if, overbooking).}
The revenue manager asks: what happens to the optimal booking limit if the cancellation rate rises by five percentage points in the peak season? Using the what-if screen, the manager lowers the show-up probability by 0.05 (from the warehouse-estimated 0.9718 to 0.9218 at H1, and from 0.9397 to 0.8897 at H2) and re-runs M4. The optimal limit rises --- \emph{the model overbooks more precisely when more bookings cancel}: at H1, $B^{*}$ moves from 192 to 202 and the overbooking level from 5 to 15 rooms; at H2, from 239 to 252 and from 13 to 26. The scenario log preserves both runs' parameters side by side for comparison.

\paragraph{Scenario S2: group request (what-if, allocation).}
A tour operator offers a block of rooms for a three-night period at 70\% of the transient rate. The manager enters the request into the allocation panel and sees the accept/reject recommendation with the expected-revenue difference and the breakeven group rate. The executed runs (Table~\ref{tab:m2demo}, Section~5.1): a 10-room request is accepted at both properties in both seasons; a \textbf{25-room request is rejected at the resort in August but accepted at the city hotel in the same week} --- the essence of per-property tactical decisions. In the low season the breakeven rate is approximately zero at both properties: with forecast demand far below capacity, the marginal rooms cost nothing, and the group is nearly free revenue --- the model makes this visible instead of leaving it to intuition.

\paragraph{Scenario S3: RevPAR target (goal seeking, pricing).}
The manager sets a target of a 20\% RevPAR increase in the low season and asks the system to find the required nightly rate. The goal-seeking screen inverts the pricing model and returns, for H1 (baseline occupancy 0.559, ADR EUR\,56.53, RevPAR EUR\,31.57), a required ADR of \textbf{EUR\,140.66 --- a 148.8\% increase}, at which occupancy falls to 0.269; for H2 (baseline 0.547, ADR EUR\,87.99, RevPAR EUR\,48.11), EUR\,218.95 (+148.8\%). The manager sees not only the number but how much it depends on an unverifiable parameter --- the honest treatment of uncertainty: with elasticity $e = -0.8$, a 20\% RevPAR target is not realistically reachable by rate alone, and the tornado quantifies why. Varying each parameter by $\pm$20\%: making the elasticity milder ($e = -0.64$) drops the required ADR to EUR\,93.80; making it more elastic ($e = -0.96$) leaves \emph{no finite solution} --- the curve turns too flat to reach the target at any price; a 20\% better baseline occupancy drops it to EUR\,56.53 (exactly the baseline ADR, since the target then requires no rate move at all); a 20\% harder target raises it to EUR\,350.05. The answer is dominated by the elasticity assumption --- which is precisely why M5 is classed under uncertainty rather than risk.

\paragraph{Scenario S4: weekly allocation with competing groups (what-if, LP).}
Several group offers compete for the same week's capacity across different room types. The LP panel shows the accepted set, the rejected set, and the bid prices per night and type. The executed run (H2, 7--13 August 2017, five offers of 12--25 rooms at EUR\,78--108): the optimal revenue is EUR\,199{,}078; the offer of 12 rooms for five nights at EUR\,108 is accepted and the other four rejected --- their rates sit below the peak-season opportunity cost. The bid prices (dual values) are positive on 28 (night, type) combinations, from EUR\,33.6 to EUR\,129.9 per room-night --- the marginal value the manager gives up by selling a room cheaply. The manager then tests what happens if one rejected group's rate is increased: the 20-room, four-night type-A offer flips from reject to accept at a \textbf{breakeven rate of EUR\,107.17}.

\paragraph{Presentation format.}
Each scenario is documented with the input parameters, the model's output, and a short interpretation --- the same format as the evidence tables in the appendix. The full parameter sets and results are stored in \texttt{fact\_scenario\_run} (87 logged runs at the time of writing, from the interface, the experiments, and the test suite alike --- all through the same registry) and can be re-loaded into the comparison screen at any time. A demonstration video (20--30 minutes) walks through all four scenarios in sequence, following the same user flow as Simon's four phases: intelligence (dashboard), design (models generate alternatives), choice (optimisation and goal seeking), and review (scenario comparison).
